# Efficient Inference and Small Language Models: A Survey

## Synthesis by theme

### 1. Scope: efficiency is a system property, not a parameter count

There is no universally agreed parameter threshold separating a small language model (SLM) from a large language model. One recent survey adopts fewer than 8B parameters as its principal scope, with exceptions, while emphasizing broad task competence rather than a narrow task-specific model as part of the distinction [1]. Other definitions are explicitly practical: SLMs are models whose reduced scale and scope lower memory and compute requirements enough to enable resource-constrained or offline deployment [2]. Thus “small” is relative to the intended device, workload, and quality threshold.

A useful taxonomy distinguishes **model-side efficiency** (training a capable smaller model or compressing an existing one), **algorithm/kernel efficiency** (doing the same computation with less data movement or more parallelism), and **serving efficiency** (sharing accelerator capacity across requests). These levers interact, but they are not interchangeable. Lower parameter count may reduce weight memory without solving long-context KV-cache growth; a faster attention kernel may not reduce autoregressive decode latency in a bandwidth-bound setup; and a quantized model may save storage without running faster unless the runtime and hardware support the relevant kernels [3][4][5][6][7].

SLMs are attractive for local and edge use because reduced memory and compute needs can support offline operation, privacy, and latency-sensitive applications [2][8]. But no one model is best for every local task: task quality must be balanced against RAM, latency, battery, thermal limits, and network constraints [9]. The central question is therefore not simply “How small can the model be?” but “Which combination of model, precision, runtime, and workload meets the required quality and service objectives on this hardware?”

### 2. Building capable small models: data, recipe, and post-training

The evidence cautions against treating parameter count as the sole predictor of capability. Phi-2 (2.7B parameters) illustrates a data-centric approach: Microsoft reports training on 1.4T tokens, including synthetic material and web data filtered for educational quality, and reports results competitive with models up to 25 times larger on some coding and mathematics evaluations. This is a provider-reported, benchmark-specific comparison, not a general claim of equivalence across tasks [10].

Phi-3-mini provides a further example of the interaction between scale and recipe. Its technical report describes a 3.8B-parameter decoder-only model trained on 3.3T tokens, with heavily filtered web and synthetic data, and reports 69% on MMLU and 8.38 on MT-bench. The report also describes a two-stage pretraining data strategy and supervised instruction tuning followed by direct preference optimization; results are benchmark-specific and should not be generalized to all tasks or deployment conditions [11][12]. The Hugging Face model card presents the 4K-context instruct checkpoint as a dense decoder-only model, describes SFT and DPO, and identifies memory- or latency-constrained deployment as an intended use [13].

Model families show differing hardware and quality choices. Gemma's technical report describes 2B and 7B decoder Transformers trained on 3T and 6T tokens, respectively; it uses multi-query attention in the 2B model and multi-head attention in the 7B model, and positions the smaller model for CPU/on-device use and the larger for efficient GPU/TPU deployment [14]. Hugging Face's Gemma 2B model card similarly presents the family as lightweight text-generation models suitable for local or cloud deployment [15]. Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct is a separate compact example: its card lists 1.54B total parameters, grouped-query attention with two KV heads, a 32,768-token context, and multilingual and structured-output capabilities as family/checkpoint features [16]. These cards describe intended capabilities and configurations; they are not directly comparable controlled tests.

Compression and distillation can also produce smaller models from larger teachers. DistilBERT, an encoder rather than a decoder-only generative SLM, reports 40% fewer parameters than BERT, 97% of BERT's GLUE performance, and 60% faster CPU inference in its batch-1 benchmark. It demonstrates the distillation principle, but its reported figures should not be read as decoder-LLM deployment measurements [17]. The broader SLM survey accordingly frames data quality, training strategy, architecture, and task as important alongside model size [1].

### 3. Compression and numerical precision

**Weight-only quantization** targets weight storage and memory bandwidth. GPTQ uses approximate second-order information for post-training quantization and reports compressing models as large as 175B to 3–4 bits per weight; its reported speedups rely on custom kernels and reduced memory movement, not fewer multiply operations [3]. AWQ uses activation statistics to identify salient channels and scale weights, without retaining a mixed-precision subset. The paper reports a 3.2–3.3× average speedup over Hugging Face FP16 across its tested models and GPUs, and reports 30 tokens/s for Llama-2-13B on an RTX 4070 with 8GB; these are specific implementation and benchmark results. The authors also note that their evaluation does not comprehensively cover robustness, fairness, toxicity, helpfulness, or calibration [18]. These results make the systems point clear: quantization changes the potential memory/compute profile, but end-to-end speed depends on kernels, hardware, calibration, and workload.

**Pruning and sparsity** seek to remove weights or structure. Wanda scores weights using their magnitude and corresponding input-activation norm; it needs a calibration forward pass but no retraining or weight update. Its authors report that large unstructured 50%-sparse LLaMA/LLaMA-2 models can match dense zero-shot accuracy in evaluated cases. Yet sparsity is not automatically acceleration: reported 2:4 structured sparsity speedups depend on hardware support, and structured sparse models without fine-tuning generally lose accuracy relative to dense models of comparable parameter count [19]. These constraints matter especially for SLMs, where a dense compact model may already fit the device and sparse kernels can add overhead.

**KV-cache compression** addresses a different memory bottleneck from weight quantization. KIVI quantizes keys per-channel and values per-token to 2 bits and reports near-baseline quality on its evaluated models, 2.6× lower peak memory including weights, and up to 4× larger batch size. Its reported throughput gains vary by workload; the method retains a full-precision residual cache and recent window, and the paper documents task-dependent accuracy risk on GSM8K for naive 2-bit quantization [5]. This is particularly relevant when long prompts or generations make KV state, rather than weights, the limiting resource.

### 4. Kernels, decoding, and memory management

FlashAttention-2 improves attention execution through tiling, work partitioning, and sequence-dimension parallelism while retaining exact attention. It reports roughly 2× speedup over FlashAttention in its studied settings, but the gains are shape- and hardware-dependent and should not be treated as a universal end-to-end decode speedup [4]. For SLM deployments, optimized kernels can matter as much as model size, but benchmark claims need to specify device, sequence length, batch, and whether prefill or decode is measured.

Speculative decoding tackles sequential token generation differently: a fast draft model proposes tokens, and the target model verifies them in parallel. The rejection-sampling method preserves the target model's output distribution without target-model retraining or architecture changes. The cited work reports 2–3× acceleration for T5-XXL in its tested settings, with gains depending on draft/target agreement and available arithmetic capacity [20]. A compact draft model may therefore complement a larger target, but speculative decoding is not guaranteed to help when extra compute is unavailable or draft acceptance is low.

Serving systems improve utilization and memory allocation across requests. PagedAttention stores KV cache in fixed-size blocks and maps logical blocks to physical blocks as generation proceeds. Its vLLM implementation reports 2–4× throughput over the compared serving systems at the same latency, with larger gains for longer sequences and larger models, although its attention kernel incurred overhead against a highly optimized comparison kernel in some measurements [6]. Orca's iteration-level scheduling allows requests to join or leave a batch at each generation step; its paper reports 36.9× throughput over FasterTransformer at the same latency on GPT-3 175B, a result tied to that system and workload [21]. Sarathi-Serve addresses prefill/decode interference by chunking prefills and scheduling them alongside ongoing decode iterations; it reports substantial capacity gains under particular strict service-level objectives, while noting that chunking and hybrid batching alone each have latency tradeoffs [22]. These systems principles remain relevant even when model sizes are small: batches and request mix determine accelerator utilization, while the best schedule depends on the latency target.

### 5. Deployment measurements and workload-aware evaluation

Evaluation should separate **prefill** from **decode**. Prefill processes the input prompt in parallel and is often compute-bound; decode generates one token at a time and is often more sensitive to memory bandwidth. Accordingly, time-to-first-token (TTFT) reflects prompt processing, while time-per-output-token (TPOT) reflects sustained generation. Hugging Face's benchmarking discussion recommends measuring these phases alongside per-user and aggregate token throughput and testing realistic prompt/output lengths, request rates, warmups, and concurrency—not just isolated tokens per second [7].

A device-level survey of 20 SLMs uses Jetson hardware and smartphones and reports latency, runtime memory, and energy measurements. In its tested setup, 4-bit quantization reduced prefill latency by an average of 50% for one quantizer, while decode-latency reductions varied from 17% to 75% across methods. It also found that model parameter count alone did not predict runtime memory: vocabulary and context/KV allocation could change the footprint substantially [23]. The result underscores why comparisons need to state precision, context size, implementation, and device.

Serving throughput has a Pareto frontier with latency rather than a universally optimal batch size. A small-model serving study reports that models up to 2.7B in its OPT experiments reached their throughput frontier on one accelerator; beyond that point, larger batches added little throughput while worsening latency. Multi-GPU serving could reduce throughput for smaller models because of transfer overhead, whereas a larger 13B model could benefit from distribution across devices [24]. Output length and KV-cache capacity also constrain the maximum batch size [24].

For edge and local use, quality, TTFT, token speed, energy/query, and cost/query should be considered together, with device capability and network availability explicit in the test [8]. One edge-first study reports that some models could not run on a Jetson Nano because of memory limits, and that TTFT increased as models approached available device memory; it also frames cloud fallback as useful when edge capacity or model capability is insufficient [8]. Hugging Face's local-model selection guidance makes a practical recommendation consistent with these results: start with the smallest model plausible for the task, then scale up only if quality is inadequate, accounting for RAM, latency, battery, and thermal constraints [9].

On-device speed claims require especially careful qualification. A Hugging Face-hosted vendor post reports Qwen3-600m at up to 75 tokens/s on some modern phones and lower rates on older devices, using a custom engine and kernels [25]. This is an implementation-specific vendor claim rather than an independent cross-platform benchmark; it illustrates the potential of specialized mobile runtimes, not a general expectation for all small models or phones.

### 6. Synthesis: practical choices and open challenges

The sources support several practical conclusions:

1. **Choose the smallest model that meets the task's quality bar, not the smallest model in isolation.** Curated and synthetic data, post-training, and architecture choices can make modest models capable, but reported benchmark parity is task- and evaluation-specific [10][11][14][16][9].
2. **Identify the actual bottleneck before optimizing.** Weight memory suggests weight quantization; long-context or high-concurrency memory pressure suggests KV-cache optimization; low accelerator utilization suggests batching and scheduling; sequential decode bottlenecks may justify speculative decoding [3][18][20][5][6][21][22].
3. **Treat speed as an end-to-end property.** Quantization and sparsity need compatible kernels; attention kernel gains may not translate to full-request gains; model size alone does not determine memory, latency, or energy [18][19][4][23][7].
4. **Benchmark the intended workload and constraints.** Report quality together with TTFT, TPOT, throughput, memory, energy, device, precision, context lengths, and request concurrency. Measure realistic task robustness and safety as well as standard language-model benchmarks [18][23][8][7].

Important open challenges include comparable device-level benchmarks across runtimes and hardware; robust quality evaluation after low-bit quantization and compression; energy measurements rather than proxy estimates; realistic long-context and multi-request tests; and scheduling approaches that meet both TTFT and per-token latency objectives. There is no universal winner: local SLMs can improve privacy, responsiveness, and offline availability, while cloud fallback or larger models remain useful when task complexity, memory, or scale exceed the edge budget [23][24][8][9].
