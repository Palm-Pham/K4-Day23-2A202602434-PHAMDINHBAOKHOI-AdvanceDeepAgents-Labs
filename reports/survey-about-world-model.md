# World Models: A Survey of Concepts, Methods, and Applications

## Synthesis by theme

### 1. What counts as a world model?

“World model” is not a single settled technical category. One useful distinction is between models intended to build internal representations of how the external world works and models intended to predict future states so an agent can simulate outcomes and make decisions. In this survey, the operational emphasis is on the latter: a model is especially useful as a world model when it predicts how a state will evolve under specified actions, rather than merely producing plausible images or descriptions [1]. This distinction matters because generative realism, physical understanding, action-conditioned prediction, and decision utility are related but not interchangeable capabilities.

The influential 2018 *World Models* formulation gives a compact example of the model-based agent loop. Its V component encodes observed frames into a compressed latent representation; its M component is a recurrent dynamics model that predicts the distribution of the next latent state from current latent information and action; and its C component is a controller trained to maximize reward. The authors showed that a controller trained in the learned model’s imagined environment could be transferred to the real environment [2]. This architecture established a recurring pattern: learn a compact state, learn how it changes, then use predicted futures to select actions.

### 2. Architectures and learning objectives

A common family of control-oriented world models learns latent state and action-conditioned dynamics, often with reward or value prediction. DreamerV3 uses a recurrent state-space model (RSSM): an encoder maps sensory inputs to stochastic latents, while recurrent dynamics predict latent representations from previous state and action. The model also predicts rewards and episode continuation and reconstructs observations; an actor and critic then improve from imagined trajectories. Its evaluation across more than 150 tasks illustrates how a shared model-based learning recipe can span diverse environments [3].

Reconstruction is not essential to every predictive model. MuDreamer removes pixel reconstruction and instead learns representations by predicting value and previously selected actions. Its authors report robustness to visual distractions and competitive Atari100k performance with faster training, while emphasizing stabilization choices such as batch normalization and KL balancing [4]. The broader design trade-off is whether a model should represent and regenerate everything in an observation, or preserve only information useful for predicting consequences and choosing actions. Reconstruction can encourage visually grounded state representations, but it can also spend modeling capacity on details irrelevant to the task [4][5].

Other systems use token prediction or video generation as their predictive mechanism. Interactive VideoGPT (iVideoGPT) serializes visual observations, actions, and rewards for autoregressive prediction, using a compressive visual tokenizer to reduce sequence length; it is pretrained on human and robot trajectories and adapted to tasks such as video prediction, visual planning, and model-based reinforcement learning [6]. Genie learns latent actions from unlabeled video and combines them with autoregressive future-frame prediction to create controllable interactive environments [7]. Pandora instead combines a pretrained language model and video model to accept natural-language actions during video-state generation [8]. These approaches expand the scale and modalities of training, but action-conditioning and visual plausibility alone do not establish reliable simulation of the real world.

### 3. Evaluation: prediction, utility, and failure modes

Evaluation should match the intended use. For a predictive model, relevant questions include whether its future states respond correctly to changes in action, remain coherent over time, represent task-relevant dynamics, and support useful planning. If the model will control an agent, downstream closed-loop success is more informative than the visual quality of an open-loop rollout alone. This is particularly important because generated imagery can look convincing without faithfully encoding dynamics or causal effects [1][9].

WorldPrediction addresses a gap between visual generation and high-level understanding by asking whether a model can identify a correct action or ordered action sequence from initial and final observations while rejecting counterfactual distractors. The benchmark uses action equivalents across different contexts to reduce shortcuts based on background continuity. Its reported top frontier-model accuracy was 57.0% on the single-action task and 38.1% on the procedural-planning task, while humans achieved perfect results in the reported setup [10]. These results illustrate why evaluating world models requires tests of causal and procedural inference, not simply whether a generated sequence resembles a plausible future.

A more complete evaluation should combine open-loop prediction metrics with downstream tests: execute a predicted plan in an environment, evaluate whether an agent benefits from imagined data, and probe action sensitivity, long-horizon consistency, and robustness to visual or state perturbations. Existing evidence also cautions against interpreting task benchmarks as direct measurements of model fidelity: a controller can sometimes compensate for imperfect prediction, while high-fidelity outputs can fail to improve decisions. Benchmark results therefore need to be read in light of both the model’s intended role and the policy or planner paired with it [3][10][5].

### 4. Embodied planning, robotics, and driving

In model-predictive control (MPC), an agent repeatedly predicts candidate futures, selects a short-horizon action sequence, executes part of it, and replans from new observations. TD-MPC learns task-oriented latent dynamics for trajectory optimization and a terminal value estimate for returns beyond the explicit planning horizon. It is motivated by the cost and compounding errors associated with modeling every detail far into the future; task-relevant latent prediction can make short-horizon planning more tractable [5].

World models have also been tested on physical robots. DayDreamer adapts Dreamer-style latent dynamics and imagined rollouts to online real-world robot learning, reporting tasks such as quadruped locomotion, object manipulation, and camera-based navigation without relying on a simulator or demonstrations in its described setup. Its authors identify accurate real-world modeling and the wear and operational burden of extended hardware training as challenges [11]. RoboDreamer takes a complementary route: it parses language instructions into action and spatial-relation primitives and composes these with video-generation models to imagine candidate robot plans. Its experiments report simulation planning results, but also note weak generalization to many real-world images and limitations associated with single-camera views and moving cameras [12].

Large-scale video pretraining is another route to embodied prediction. V-JEPA 2 is pretrained on more than one million hours of internet video; its action-conditioned V-JEPA 2-AC stage uses less than 62 hours of unlabeled robot video and predicts future representations conditioned on actions. The paper reports zero-shot deployment on Franka arms in two labs not represented in the robot-video training data [13]. This demonstrates a promising transfer pathway, not a general guarantee of reliable robot control. More generally, long-horizon planning remains challenging because model inaccuracies and computational cost constrain how far and how broadly agents can search over futures [8][9].

In autonomous driving, world models can predict future scenes or latent states under alternative driving intentions and support trajectory selection. World4Drive combines intention-aware future latent prediction with a selector that ranks candidate trajectories, and reports results on both open-loop nuScenes and closed-loop NavSim evaluations [14]. GAIA-1 combines an autoregressive transformer and video diffusion decoder to generate diverse driving futures conditioned on video, text, and actions. Its authors also warn that visually convincing generative video may not learn the dynamics required for precise prediction and robust decisions, and report that the generation process was not real-time [9]. These examples show the value of simulation and counterfactual scene prediction, while underscoring the need for closed-loop evaluation and explicit safety testing.

### 5. Generative and interactive world models

Generative world models use video or image synthesis to make imagined environments directly inspectable and, in some cases, controllable. Genie learns latent actions from unlabeled internet video and predicts future frame tokens, enabling user interaction with generated platform-game-like environments. The paper also notes limits including unrealistic futures, short visual memory, reduced long-horizon consistency, and slow generation at the time of publication [7]. GameNGen demonstrates a more task-specific form: a diffusion model conditioned on recent frames and actions generates the next DOOM frame. The authors report interactive-rate output and multi-minute sessions, while explicitly characterizing the result as an imperfect game-specific simulation rather than an exact engine [15].

Foundation-model approaches aim to reuse large pretrained video or language models. iVideoGPT brings observations, actions, and rewards into a common autoregressive sequence [6], while Pandora permits natural-language actions during video generation [8]. Dynamic World Simulation adapts pretrained video generators with action-conditioning and a motion-focused loss, and adds prioritized imagination for model-based reinforcement learning. Its authors emphasize that pretrained visual priors do not automatically provide precise action conditioning or accurate dynamic transitions [16]. VRAG addresses long-horizon drift through video retrieval and explicit global-state conditioning; it reports improved temporal consistency and points to compute and memory limits as well as ethical concerns around realistic misleading or manipulated video [17].

Interactive environment generators such as Genie 2 and Genie 3 move toward promptable, playable scenes. DeepMind describes Genie 2 as generating action-controllable 3D environments from a single image prompt, with interactions and predicted behavior of other agents [18]. Its Genie 3 announcement reports real-time text-prompted environments, but notes limitations in interaction duration, action space, multi-agent interaction, geographic accuracy, and text legibility; it also describes safety and responsibility as open concerns [19]. These are important demonstrations of controllable generation and potential environments for agent training, but their reported capabilities should not be conflated with general-purpose, physically accurate simulators.

### 6. Limitations, safety, and research priorities

Across latent-control and video-generative approaches, the core technical challenge is not merely producing a plausible next observation; it is maintaining a useful, action-sensitive model of state evolution over the horizon that matters to the task. Autoregressive errors can accumulate, long video contexts are expensive, candidate-action search scales poorly, and models can omit unobserved or task-critical state. Reports on robot, driving, and video models point to further constraints in real-world transfer, camera geometry, physical consistency, and real-time operation [11][12][13][9][16][17].

Safety evaluation must also address what the model imagines and what the agent does with those predictions. A review of embodied world models organizes safety concerns around prediction faults and pathologies, and notes that many evaluations cover a limited set of instructions or depend on simulator-provided state [20]. For generative systems, realistic video raises additional concerns about misleading or manipulated content, while interactive environments create questions about unsafe actions and untested situations [17][19]. The sources therefore support testing both the predictive model and its downstream policy, including counterfactuals, distribution shifts, failure recovery, physical constraints, and high-impact scenarios.

The strongest overall research direction is to treat world models as components in a closed loop rather than as image generators judged in isolation. Progress calls for better structured state representations, reliable action conditioning, calibrated uncertainty and failure detection, efficient long-horizon planning, and evaluations that connect perception and prediction to actual task outcomes. The evidence surveyed here suggests that no single architecture is yet universally best: latent predictive models often align naturally with control, while generative video models offer intuitive rich rollouts and scalable visual priors. Their value will depend on measurable predictive and decision utility for the intended environment—not on realism alone [1][10][5][13][16].
