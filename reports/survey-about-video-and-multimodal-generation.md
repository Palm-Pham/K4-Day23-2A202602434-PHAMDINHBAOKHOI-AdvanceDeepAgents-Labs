# Survey of Video and Multimodal Generation

## Synthesis by theme

### 1. From short clips to increasingly controllable video generation

Recent video-generation systems have converged around two broad model families: latent-space diffusion, commonly using Transformer denoisers, and autoregressive prediction over discrete visual tokens. Designs also differ in whether they synthesize the temporal sequence jointly or build it through sparse keyframes and temporal refinement. The shared challenge is to represent appearance, motion, and scene changes coherently while keeping generation tractable as resolution and duration grow.

Lumiere is an example of joint space-time generation: its Space-Time U-Net processes spatial and temporal dimensions together and generates the full temporal duration in one model pass, followed by spatial super-resolution. Its reported base output is 80 frames at 16 fps, and the same framework supports image-to-video, inpainting, and stylization. The authors contrast its temporal strategy with cascaded keyframe approaches, which can alias rapid motion or lose global context across temporal upsampling stages. Lumiere also reports limits in text adherence, complex motion, clip duration, and memory-heavy high-resolution upsampling. [1]

Sora’s public technical report presented a diffusion approach that can generate up to a minute of high-fidelity video and described camera movement, object persistence through occlusion, and multiple shots of a character as observed strengths. The report also explicitly notes failures in basic physical interactions, object-state updates, and long-sample coherence. These disclosures illustrate the distinction between visually convincing samples and robust simulation of the underlying world. [2]

Autoregressive token models offer a different route to unified generation and conditioning. VideoPoet uses a decoder-only Transformer trained on tokenized text, image, video, and audio; a shared model can be adapted to text-to-video, image-to-video, editing, stylization, extension, and audio-conditioned tasks. This breadth comes with a token-efficiency problem: the paper’s example of a 17-frame 896×512 video expands to 35,840 tokens, making direct high-resolution autoregressive sampling costly. Its pipeline therefore adds a non-autoregressive super-resolution stage. [3]

Large diffusion-Transformer systems have pushed scale, quality, resolution, and task breadth. Meta’s Movie Gen report describes a 30-billion-parameter video model, Flow Matching, upsampling to 1080p, and generation up to 16 seconds at 16 fps; the model family also includes synchronized audio generation, personalization from a person’s image, and instruction-based editing. Its claimed comparisons against commercial systems are the authors’ evaluation results rather than a universal ranking. [4] HunyuanVideo describes an open 13-billion-parameter spatiotemporal latent diffusion Transformer with Flow Matching and a unified image/video attention design. Its human comparisons report favorable results against selected systems, but these too are author-reported evaluations. [5] Wan likewise reports open 1.3B and 14B models and support for text-to-video, image-to-video, editing, personalization, real-time video, and audio generation; the authors report the 1.3B version at 8.19 GB VRAM for 480p generation. [6]

Scaling duration exposes architectural cost. LinGen proposes replacing quadratic-cost DiT self-attention with a Mamba-and-temporal-attention hybrid, reporting minute-length examples and lower compute and latency than its DiT-4B comparison in the authors’ tests. The paper reports a 68-second, 512p example and up to 15× lower FLOPs and 11.5× lower latency for that setting; these are reported benchmark results, not a general guarantee across workloads. [7] In parallel, controllability work such as Boximator adds hard and soft bounding-box motion constraints to existing video diffusion systems, targeting explicit object paths rather than relying on text prompts alone. The Hugging Face Daily Papers summary reports improved box alignment and human preference versus base models. [8]

### 2. Multimodal generation: joint audio-video, speech, and structured worlds

The field is moving beyond silent video toward systems that generate or condition on multiple synchronized streams. Three recurring designs are visible in the research: a single autoregressive sequence of modality-specific tokens, separate modality branches joined by adapters or cross-attention, and shared multimodal training that can work with missing modalities. Each trades off simplicity and unified conditioning against specialization, data requirements, and temporal alignment.

VideoPoet exemplifies a single-stream approach, representing image, video, and audio with modality-specific tokens. Its task suite includes audio-to-video, video-to-audio, and audio-video continuation, in addition to video-only tasks; the model’s continuation task predicts future frames and matching audio from an initial audiovisual segment. Its use of compressed, quantized visual tokens can constrain fine visual detail, especially on small objects and during substantial motion. [3]

Joint diffusion systems often retain modality-specific processing while adding explicit links between streams. SyncFlow uses separate video and audio Transformer towers with a modality adaptor, and a staged training recipe that first trains video, then audio, then jointly fine-tunes the two. It reports text-conditioned synchronized video and audio generation, as well as video-to-audio synthesis. [9] MMAudio instead trains a multimodal Transformer with video-audio data alongside larger audio-text collections, masking absent modalities; its synchronization module uses high-frame-rate visual features. The authors report better audio quality, semantic alignment, and synchronization. They frame the system around general audio effects/Foley rather than human speech, and note limits from scarce or noisy paired audiovisual data; it should therefore not be treated as a speech-synthesis benchmark. [10]

JavisDiT adds a learned spatiotemporal synchronization prior to joint audio-video diffusion. Its report describes training on 0.6 million synchronized text-video-audio triplets and a benchmark focused on complex sounding scenes. It identifies simultaneous events and multiple sound sources as difficult alignment cases, and notes substantial inference cost and evaluation limitations. [11] For speech tied to a visible speaker, Visatronic instead uses a decoder-only Transformer to jointly represent video, text, and speech as temporally aligned token streams. The paper evaluates on VoxCeleb2 and LRS3 and introduces a phoneme-level temporal-alignment measure, while describing different modality sampling rates and sparse text as challenges. [12]

Multimodality also includes structured 3D and embodied generation, where the goal is not just a plausible rendered clip but a controllable scene with meaningful geometry and interactions. C3V uses a multimodal LLM to decompose text into scene, object, and motion concepts, assign expert models, and refine a 3D-aware composition. The paper reports concept-level editing and viewpoint control, while limiting its current concept coverage and acknowledging that its priors are not physically grounded. [13] PhyScene uses diffusion with collision, room-layout, reachability, and interactivity guidance to generate physically interactable indoor scenes for embodied AI. Its authors stress that common realism metrics miss physical plausibility and that its own coverage is limited by room types and object classes. [14] Together, these efforts suggest a spectrum from pixel-level video synthesis toward generative systems that expose scene structure and support downstream interaction.

### 3. Evaluation: from visual quality to faithfulness and human alignment

No single metric captures video-generation quality. Benchmarks increasingly break it into dimensions: appearance and technical quality, identity consistency, motion smoothness, temporal flicker, spatial relations, prompt adherence, physical plausibility, and commonsense. VBench organizes evaluation into 16 dimensions and uses human-preference data to examine the relationship between automated measurements and human judgments. Its authors caution that existing metrics do not fully reflect human perception and that the benchmark did not then cover safety or equality. [15]

The newer VBench++ extends coverage to image-to-video and includes trustworthiness dimensions such as cultural fairness, human bias, and safety. Its safety protocol combines multiple classifiers and flags a video as unsafe when more than half its frames are flagged, showing how frame-level checks can be assembled into a video-level decision. [16] VBench-2.0 shifts further toward intrinsic faithfulness, evaluating human fidelity, controllability, creativity, physics, and commonsense across 18 capabilities with VLM/LLM judgments, specialized detectors, and human-preference validation. Its authors still report weaknesses in complex plots, object-state changes, and stable commonsense reasoning. [17]

Human-aligned evaluation is an important counterweight to automatic scores. Video-Bench uses iterative multimodal-LLM questioning and few-shot scoring, and reports higher correlation with human preferences than prior methods in its experiments; its benchmark includes 419 prompts and 35,196 human evaluations. [18] T2VEval provides another diagnostic view with 148 prompts and 1,783 videos from 13 systems, rating overall impression, text-video consistency, realness, and technical quality. It reports realness as a weaker dimension and identifies errors in physical rules, body structure, and movement; its comparison also cautions that Sora’s rank used only 48 public demos. [19] These benchmarks make comparisons more interpretable, but reported rankings remain conditional on prompts, sampled outputs, model versions, and evaluator design.

Evaluation needs to match the modality and use case. For generated audio-video, synchronization, event-to-sound correspondence, intelligible speech, and cross-modal consistency matter in addition to visual quality. For 3D and embodied generation, physical plausibility and interaction affordances are essential rather than optional aesthetic dimensions. The reviewed papers also show why human validation remains necessary: automated VLM/LLM judges can scale scoring, but benchmark authors report issues with evaluator consistency, video understanding, and cross-modal reasoning. [11][14][17][18]

### 4. Deployment, safety, and rights

Deployment is expanding through hosted services as well as open model releases. Google’s Vertex AI announcement positions Veo 3 and a faster variant for enterprise and developer use, with single-step video-plus-audio generation, dialogue, lip-sync, and sound effects. [20] OpenAI’s Sora 2 system card describes a video-and-audio model with synchronized audio and enhanced steerability, deployed initially through limited invitations with restrictions on photorealistic-person and video uploads and stronger safeguards for content involving minors. [21] Such rollout choices are part of the product design: moderation, access control, and the available input pathways shape which uses are enabled and which risks are exposed.

Provenance measures aim to make generated media easier to identify and trace. OpenAI says Sora 2 outputs launched with visible watermarks and embedded C2PA metadata, alongside internal reverse-image and audio search for tracing outputs. [22] These are complements to moderation rather than proof that all synthetic content can be reliably detected or that downstream misuse is prevented. Video-SafetyBench highlights risks particular to temporal and multimodal inputs: its evaluations find video-text compositional attacks, including benign-looking queries whose unsafe implication comes from the video, and report higher attack success for video inputs than static images in its test setting. [23]

Rights and identity risks are not confined to model safeguards. The reviewed UK government report identifies training-data transparency, licensing, technical controls, enforcement, and realistic non-consensual replicas of a person’s face or voice as policy issues. It notes that labeling can help users identify synthetic media but describes no UK-wide labeling obligation in the report’s account. [24] OECD guidance identifies identity fraud, non-consensual manipulation, and disinformation among deepfake risks, and recommends consent, transparency, accountability, and authenticity verification, including provenance approaches such as watermarking and C2PA. [25] Copyright questions also remain relevant to generated video and audio: the reviewed U.S. Copyright Office report says protection turns on human authorship and the nature and extent of human expressive contribution, assessed case by case. This makes prompting, source material, editing, and arrangement consequential rather than treating all model outputs as having the same authorship status. [26]

### 5. Cross-cutting limitations and research directions

Across the sources, several bottlenecks recur. First, temporal and causal coherence remain hard: models can produce convincing frames but fail at object permanence, physical interactions, fast motion, and long-horizon continuity. Second, compute and representation constrain duration and resolution; autoregressive token sequences grow large, while diffusion Transformers incur substantial spatiotemporal attention and sampling costs. Efficient architectures, compressed latents, staged pipelines, and lower-cost variants address these constraints, but each introduces tradeoffs in detail, context, latency, or quality. [2][1][3][7]

Third, multimodal systems need more and better aligned data. Paired video-audio data can be scarce, noisy, or contaminated by non-diegetic sound; complex scenes make it difficult to associate the right event with the right audio at the right time. Speech, sound effects, and music have different generation demands; the cited MMAudio system focuses on general effects/Foley, whereas speech-centric systems such as Visatronic address a distinct task. [9][10][11][12] Fourth, control and grounding need to become more structured: box-based trajectories, synchronized token streams, explicit 3D representations, and physical constraints provide stronger controls than text alone, but available concepts, durations, object coverage, and world dynamics remain limited. [8][12][13][14]

Finally, measurement and governance must evolve alongside capability. Benchmarks need to test realness, prompt faithfulness, physics, human fidelity, safety, fairness, provenance, and modality synchronization without overclaiming from limited samples or automated judge scores. Deployment safeguards need to address temporal and compositional harms, non-consensual likenesses, privacy, minors, and copyright as well as static-image safety. Taken together, the evidence points to a field transitioning from making plausible clips toward controllable, synchronized, longer-form media generation—but not yet to dependable world simulation or universally reliable production workflows. [15][16][17][18][23][21][22][24][25][19]
